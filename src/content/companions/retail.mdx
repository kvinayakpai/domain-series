## Tests that pass

Twelve tests, the kind a dbt project ships on day one: uniqueness, nulls, accepted values, relationships, and reconciliations. The strongest is the book stock against its own ledger:

```sql
-- close = last close + receipts - sales - write-offs + adjustments
WITH rcv AS (
  SELECT date, sku, sum(u) AS u FROM (
    SELECT CAST(received_ts AS DATE) AS date, sku_booked AS sku,
           units_booked AS u FROM goods_receipts
    UNION ALL
    SELECT CAST(delivery_ts AS DATE), sku, units FROM fresh_deliveries)
  GROUP BY ALL),
sold AS (SELECT CAST(bill_ts AS DATE) AS date, sku_scanned AS sku,
                sum(qty) AS u FROM pos_lines GROUP BY ALL),
wo AS (SELECT date, sku, sum(units) AS u FROM write_offs GROUP BY ALL),
adj AS (SELECT CAST(count_ts AS DATE) AS date, sku, sum(adjustment) AS u
        FROM stock_counts WHERE hour(count_ts) < 22 GROUP BY ALL)
SELECT b.date, b.sku, b.book_on_hand_close AS book,
       prev.book_on_hand_close + coalesce(rcv.u, 0) - coalesce(sold.u, 0)
         - coalesce(wo.u, 0) + coalesce(adj.u, 0) AS rebuilt
FROM inventory_book b
JOIN inventory_book prev ON prev.sku = b.sku AND prev.date = b.date - 1
LEFT JOIN rcv  ON rcv.date  = b.date AND rcv.sku  = b.sku
LEFT JOIN sold ON sold.date = b.date AND sold.sku = b.sku
LEFT JOIN wo   ON wo.date   = b.date AND wo.sku   = b.sku
LEFT JOIN adj  ON adj.date  = b.date AND adj.sku  = b.sku
WHERE book <> rebuilt;
```

It returns no rows, for all eleven items on every day. The book is exactly what its ledger says it is, which is the problem: a ledger cannot see a bay. The notebook goes one step further and rebuilds the book hour by hour from receipts, sales and counts; the rebuild matches every close, and it is the `hourly_book` the domain tests and the detector use.

## Finding the censored hours

A till that records nothing for an hour might mean nobody wanted the item. It might mean nobody could find it. The way to tell is to ask how unlikely the silence is. If an item usually sells at a known rate, the chance of a run of open hours with no sales is the Poisson probability of zero: e raised to minus the sales expected over the run. When that falls below a threshold while the books show stock, the run is probably a gap.

The detector learns each item's pattern from May, before the story starts: the share of a day's sales in each open hour, and how weekdays and weekends compare with the average day. The expected sales in an hour are last night's forecast times that pattern.

```python
import math
import numpy as np

def learn_patterns(may):
    """may[sku]: May's till units, shape (28 days, 15 open hours)."""
    weekend = np.array([d.weekday() >= 5 for d in MAY_DAYS])
    out = {}
    for sku, a in may.items():
        per_day = a.sum(axis=1)
        out[sku] = {
            # share of a day's sales in each open hour, lightly smoothed
            "profile": (a.sum(axis=0) + 0.5) / (a.sum() + 0.5 * 15),
            "weekday": per_day[~weekend].mean() / per_day.mean(),
            "weekend": per_day[weekend].mean() / per_day.mean(),
        }
    return out

def detect(sales, book, forecast, patterns, alpha=0.001):
    """Runs of open hours with no sales while the books show stock,
    flagged once the chance of no sales falls below alpha."""
    limit, runs = -math.log(alpha), []
    for sku, p in patterns.items():
        run, expected, flagged, lone = [], 0.0, None, False
        for d, day in enumerate(JUNE_DAYS):
            level = p["weekend"] if day.weekday() >= 5 else p["weekday"]
            for h in range(15):
                units = sales[sku][d, h]
                e = forecast[sku][d] * level * p["profile"][h]
                if flagged and units == 1 and not lone:
                    lone = True                 # one lone scan inside a gap
                    run.append((d, h, e, 1))
                    expected += e
                    continue
                if units > 0 or book[sku][d, h] <= 0:
                    if flagged:                 # a sale, or the books know
                        runs.append(flagged | {"hours": run})
                    run, expected, flagged, lone = [], 0.0, None, False
                    continue
                run.append((d, h, e, 0))
                expected += e
                if flagged is None and expected > limit:  # P(0) < alpha
                    flagged = {"sku": sku, "start": run[0][:2],
                               "flag": (d, h + 1)}
        if flagged:
            runs.append(flagged | {"hours": run})
    return runs
```

Three details matter. The forecast is the store's own, made the night before, so the detector uses nothing the store did not have. Overnight closure does not end a run, so a gap that starts at 16:00 carries into the next morning. And the lone-scan rule comes from looking at the data: at 09:30 on Thursday 11 June the tills recorded one 5 kg bag in the middle of the gap, and the answer key says it was a 1 kg bag scanned as 5 kg. Inside a long silence, one unit is as likely to be a scan error as a sale.

Scored against the answer key, an alert counts as real if the shelf was empty when it fired, and a gap counts as found if an alert fired inside it. Only gaps of three open hours or more are counted, since a shorter one ends before anyone could act.

<DetectorTable />

At 1 in 1,000 the detector raises 10 alerts in June and 6 are real. The 4 that are not, for sugar, oil twice and 1 kg atta, came in runs of five to eight quiet open hours with the shelf stocked. Real demand is noisier than a Poisson process: some days an item simply sells less, so long silences happen more often than the model expects. A detector that allows for that would raise fewer false alarms; that is exercise three. The column on the right comes from rerunning June in the simulation with someone checking the shelf within an hour of each alert, on the same shoppers. It is the number the store would feel.

## Unconstraining demand

The store's forecast is the 28-day mean of recorded sales. The fix keeps the rule and changes its input: add to each day the sales expected in its censored hours. Those are the runs the detector flagged, and the hours the books show no stock, which the detector skips because the records already know.

```python
# daily[sku]: units the tills recorded per day, May and June
unconstrained = {sku: daily[sku].copy() for sku in daily}
for r in runs:
    for d, h, expected, recorded in r["hours"]:
        day = len(MAY_DAYS) + d
        unconstrained[r["sku"]][day] += max(0.0, expected - recorded)
# hours with no stock on the books are censored too
for sku, d, h in stockouts:
    gap = expected_sales(sku, d, h) - sales[sku][d, h]
    unconstrained[sku][len(MAY_DAYS) + d] += max(0.0, gap)
# the store's rule, a 28-day mean, now on demand instead of sales
forecast_aware = {s: rolling_mean(a, 28) for s, a in unconstrained.items()}
```

Scoring needs care, because the obvious target is censored too.

<ForecastErrors />

Against recorded sales the store's forecast looks 13% too high, and a tuning loop scored that way would push it down; scored the same way, the unconstrained forecast looks worse still, 22% high. Against the shoppers who came for 5 kg atta the store's forecast is 8% too low, and the unconstrained version is 1% low. Across all seven warehouse items the bias falls from −3.7% to −1.5%: most items had only short gaps, and the forecast only changes where the data was censored.

On 28 June the unconstrained forecast stands at 25.6 bags a day, a little above the 24.9 shoppers came for. Some shoppers who found the shelf empty came back later and bought: the imputation counts them once in the gap, and the tills count them again on their return. Over June the unconstrained series comes to 716 bags. The answer key has 717 bags asked for, counting every visit, and 698 counting each shopper once. The estimate recovers the asks, not the shoppers; netting out the returns is a good extension.

<CensoredForecast />

The same fix applies to anything trained on sales. A price elasticity estimated across these weeks would read the empty shelf as shoppers leaving at the same price. A pack-mix model would read it as a shift to 1 kg. The [FreshRetailNet-50K dataset](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16319) (2025) shows the same bias in hourly fresh-food sales, where recovering the censored demand cut a systematic underestimate from 7.37% to near zero; Chen and Mersereau (2015) survey the methods for censored demand and record inaccuracy in stores.

## Availability, five definitions

On-shelf availability is a metric that only means something once you define it. In a semantic layer that is a choice someone makes, usually without seeing the alternatives side by side:

```yaml
metrics:
  - name: osa_book            # 97% for 5 kg atta in June
    description: Open hours with at least one unit in book stock
    measure: avg(hourly_book.book > 0)
  - name: osa_audit           # 75%, from 8 audits
    description: Shelf audits that found the item on the shelf
    measure: avg(shelf_audits.status = 'on shelf')
  - name: osa_books_tills     # 79%
    description: Open hours with stock on the books, outside the
      gaps the till lines show
    measure: 1 - count(censored_hours) / count(open_hours)
```

<AvailabilityWays />

The book says 97%. The answer key says 77% of open hours had a bag on the shelf, and 78% of the bags shoppers wanted were there. The two record-based measures that come closest use an independent observation (the audits) or a model of the observation process (the tills). Neither appears in the store's dashboard.

## Two scripted agents

The post hands the KPI to a replenishment agent. Like the assistant, the agent is scripted, so every run can be repeated: the rules below stand in for a language model's decisions. It runs inside the world's simulator rather than on the exported records, because its actions change what happens next: a check finds the cases, the shelf fills, sales resume, the next order changes. Each night at 22:00, before the store's ordering run, it does this for every warehouse item:

```python
# demand and availability, as each agent reads them
if agent == "naive":
    demand = recorded_sales                                     # per day
    available = share_of_open_hours(book_stock > 0, last_3_days)
else:  # the domain rules
    demand = recorded_sales + expected_sales_in_censored_hours  # per day
    available = 1 - censored_open_hours(last_3_days) / open_hours(last_3_days)

# the rule both agents share
if sum(demand[-3:]) < 0.5 * sum(forecasts_that_drove[-3:]) and available >= 0.95:
    set_forecast(item, mean(demand[-7:]), nights=7)             # "demand has dropped"

# the domain agent's extra rule: in stock means on the shelf
if agent == "domain":
    run = open_run_of_no_sales_while_books_show_stock(item)     # spans closing time
    if run and exp(-run.expected_sales) < 1 / 1000 or audit_found_empty_today(item):
        ask_for_shelf_check(item, at="08:00 tomorrow")          # a thorough count, then refill
```

Censored hours are the detector's: open hours with no stock on the books, and runs of no sales while the books show stock once a run that long is less likely than 1 in 1,000 under the item's usual hourly rate, the flagged run counted whole. Expected sales come from the item's May pattern and last night's forecast, as above. A check ends the run it was asked about. Otherwise both agents leave the store's forecast, the 28-day mean of recorded sales, alone. The code is `review()` in the world's [simulator]({worldCode}); `Scenario(agent="naive")` or `Scenario(agent="domain")` reruns June with each.

<AgentOutcomes />

One June is one draw, and agents' actions interact with the timing of everything after them: the first agent's smaller order moved the wrong pallet to a day later, which shortened that gap by luck. So the comparison is repeated on forty more Junes, seeds 1 to 40, with different shoppers and the same planted failures, each agent on the same draws as no agent. The first agent's cuts change little on average: while the books show stock, the reorder rule is not ordering anyway. The second agent's checks reach the cases the books cannot.

## Same store, different item: milk

Milk is censored every evening it sells out. Pouch milk arrives at 05:30 on a standing order and is good for 48 hours, so the store sees sales up to the moment the crate empties and nothing after. Choosing the order is a newsvendor problem: order too much and pouches expire, too little and shoppers leave without milk. Both sides of the trade need demand above the sell-out point, which the tills never record. Here is the trade, from the answer key:

<MilkTable />

The order that earns the most on milk itself is 5% below today's, and the records can show that: sales and expired pouches are both recorded. What they cannot show is the other column, the pouches shoppers wanted and did not find, and whether those shoppers took the rest of their basket elsewhere. Estimating demand above the sell-out from the time each day's last pouch sold is exercise four.

## How to learn this domain, for data people

1. **How stock moves in a store.** Receiving, put-away, backroom, shelf, counts; book against physical. Read [Rekik and others (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1111/jbl.70079) for how wrong records get, then Chen and Mersereau (2015) for the analytics that compensate.
2. **Forecasting, and scoring it honestly.** [Forecasting: Principles and Practice](https://otexts.com/fpp3/) (Hyndman and Athanasopoulos, free online) for the methods and their evaluation. Then [FreshRetailNet-50K](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16319) (Wang and others, 2025), a public dataset of hourly sales with every stockout marked.
3. **Data quality past the schema.** dbt's [data tests](https://docs.getdbt.com/docs/build/data-tests) check that data agrees with itself. Write the second kind as well: tests that encode how the world works, like the four above.
4. **Giving an assistant the domain.** The BIRD benchmark (Li and others, 2023) for what text-to-SQL gets wrong, and [dbt Labs' 2026 benchmark](https://docs.getdbt.com/blog/semantic-layer-vs-text-to-sql-2026) for what a semantic layer adds.
5. **Practice.** Store 014's records and the notebook here; then the [M5 data](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy) from Walmart, which has unit sales, prices and a calendar but no stock records. Find the runs of zeros that are stock-outs, not demand.
