I have been very fortunate and blessed in my career as I got exposed to the importance of domain knowledge pretty early.

My first data warehousing project provided exposure to CPG (Consumer Packaged Goods). When I entered the project, the data mart for the Detergents business was built and I was tasked with coding the KPIs in PL/SQL. I used to take pride in being very good with the Oracle database and wrote the code for the KPIs. Pretty soon I realized that writing code was easy, but ensuring that the code took care of all business scenarios, and that the final KPI values were 100% accurate all the time, was much more difficult. The joke about me in those days was "Vinayak hardly works, his code does all the hard work" – that was because I would automate the whole process of testing and walk around while the code was working hard.

After few weeks, I was asked to replicate the KPI writing process for the Beverages business. I was asked to come up with a plan and estimate the time it would take to implement the KPIs. When I came up with the plan, I was asked to defend the numbers in front of the Data leader in the client business. I don't know how my manager entrusted that responsibility to a young engineer with all of 3 years of work experience.

A quick detour. One of my favorite lines over the years is "I found myself saying". That's because I find myself listening to what comes out of me at times and get amazed, wondering that I never knew that, that's how I thought. Which is why I often say, "I found myself saying".

Coming back to the main narrative, the Data leader was not convinced as to why replicating the KPIs written for Detergents for Beverages would take so much time. Then "I found myself saying" – it's not about the code, but the Business Value. You have spent so much effort in building this data mart, and the real use of this would be when business users use these KPIs for taking key business decisions. It's important that these numbers are 100% accurate, all the time, every time.

**So the effort is for testing every business scenario and ensuring that trusted business value is delivered.**

Another quick detour. I don't believe that the above narration is 100% accurate. As a keen student of Neuroscience, I do understand our memories are mostly made up, but the fact is that the Data Leader was convinced of my response. Getting an opportunity to work on implementing KPIs for other Data Marts gave me a feeling that I might have had a valid argument.

My journey with Domain Knowledge continued in further engagements with that client and beyond. I had learnt the trick of speaking business language, and that allowed me to converse with customers of different nationalities and ethnicities with ease. This gave me tremendous exposure and allowed me to play varied roles.

Fast forward into the Agentic AI era. When I wanted to be an AI builder, domain was a natural choice and I ended up building a domain explorer solution covering over 100 domains. That also opened my eyes to how little I understood. I was using AI to build domain knowledge, but then does AI have domain knowledge? When AI is tasked to act on behalf of people, what mistakes would it make, and how can humans catch it? What's the real domain knowledge, beyond KPI definitions?

## Store 014: my first attempt at an answer

To start finding answers, few weeks back I built a supermarket. It exists only in a simulation, and I call it Store 014. It is a supermarket in Bengaluru, and because I built the simulation, I know what really happened on every shelf, truck and payment in June 2026. That means I can check each of its numbers against reality, much like I once had to defend mine in front of that Data leader.

Let me start with three KPIs from Store 014 for June, each measured twice: as the store's systems reported it, and as it really was.

<KpiPairs ids={['osa', 'otif', 'success-rate']} />

In those 90 minutes on Saturday evening, 15 customers paid twice.

Each number was computed correctly. 31 of the store's 32 data tests passed, and the other one warned about milk.

Each is still wrong about what it claims to measure. Why? Because each comes from a record that cannot see that thing: book stock cannot see the shelf, a shipping notice cannot see inside a pallet, and a till cannot see the customer's bank.

**Knowing what a record cannot see is domain knowledge.**

I believe it is the most useful thing a data or AI team can bring to the age of AI agents. It is the same lesson the Data leader taught me, only now the one acting on the numbers may be an AI agent. Put simply, in the agentic era domain knowledge is key, so data and AI teams should focus on domain KPIs.

## Why it matters more now

A person who reads a wrong KPI might question it. An AI agent acts on it.

Let me share what happened in the simulation. A scripted replenishment agent read Store 014's records every night and adjusted its forecasts. When 5 kg atta stopped selling, it saw 100% availability, decided demand had dropped and cut the forecast. The shelf had been empty since the previous afternoon.

I then gave the same agent one more rule, that in stock means on the shelf. It asked for a shelf check instead, and across forty simulated Junes 5 kg atta lost half as many sales.

Is this only my simulation talking? Outside this series, the evidence points the same way.

- As per [a 2026 benchmark by dbt Labs](https://docs.getdbt.com/blog/semantic-layer-vs-text-to-sql-2026), current AI models answered 65% of business questions about an insurance database correctly from the raw tables, and 98–100% once the business's terms and rules were written down as data models and a semantic layer.
- [MIT's NANDA initiative found that about 95% of organizations saw no measurable return](https://virtualizationreview.com/articles/2025/08/19/mit-report-finds-most-ai-business-investments-fail-reveals-genai-divide.aspx) from generative AI, and traced it to systems that do not adapt to their context or fit the way people work.

## Two numbers for every KPI

The fix this series argues for is small: give every KPI that matters two numbers, what the system reports and what an independent check observes. The KPI page does this for all eighteen of the series' KPIs.

Then track how long the gap takes to find. At Store 014, joining records the store already had found each gap within hours, and waiting for someone to notice took days.
