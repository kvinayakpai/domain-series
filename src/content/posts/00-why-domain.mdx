Three numbers from one fictional supermarket in Bengaluru, Store 014, in the synthetic world behind this series. Availability of 5 kg atta in June: 97%. Deliveries on time and in full: 92%. UPI payments approved at the till on a busy Saturday evening: 61%.

Each number is computed correctly, from records a data pipeline would call healthy. Each is also wrong about what it claims to measure. The shelf had 5 kg atta 77% of the time. 84% of deliveries brought the right cases on time. 82% of those UPI payments reached the store's bank, and 15 customers paid twice.

<ThreeKpis />

None of these gaps is a data-quality problem in the usual sense. Nothing is missing, duplicated or malformed. Each KPI is computed from a record that cannot see what the KPI is about: book stock cannot see the shelf, a shipping notice cannot see inside a pallet, and a till cannot see a customer's bank account. Knowing what a record cannot see is domain knowledge. This series argues it is the most important thing a data or AI team brings to the agentic era.

## Why it matters more now

Until recently, a misleading KPI made a misleading slide. Someone in the room might know the store, ask about the backroom, and catch it.

Agents act. A replenishment agent reading the atta records does not present a chart; it trims the order. A support agent reading the till does not flag an anomaly; it tells the customer the payment failed. And agents act on their own results. An agent that cuts the order because sales fell creates the next empty shelf, which lowers sales again, which the next forecast learns. A wrong number stops being an error in a report and becomes a loop in operations.

The evidence that context, not the model, now limits these systems keeps growing.

**Models raised the floor; domain modelling made it reliable.** In April 2026 dbt Labs reran a 2023 benchmark of business questions over an insurance database. In 2023, GPT-4 answered 33% of them correctly from the raw tables; in 2026, current models answered 65%. Adding three data models that encoded how the business works lifted that to 84–90%, and a semantic layer reached 98–100%.

**Following the business's rules every time is the hard part.** When Sierra launched τ-bench, which tests agents serving customers under a retailer's and an airline's policies, the best model solved fewer than half the tasks. In retail it got the same task right on all eight tries only about a quarter of the time. Scores have climbed since, but production asks the same question: does the agent apply the domain's rules consistently?

**Most enterprise AI falls short for domain reasons.** MIT's NANDA initiative found that about 95% of organisations saw no measurable return from generative AI, and traced it to systems that do not adapt to context or fit the workflow. Gartner expects more than 40% of agentic AI projects to be cancelled by the end of 2027, citing costs, unclear business value and weak risk controls. Choosing the decision, the KPI and the guardrail is domain work.

**In a real shop, structure beat intelligence.** In Anthropic's Project Vend, Claude ran a small shop in the company's office. In the first phase it sold items below cost, was talked into discounts and invented payment details. In the second, with a customer-management system, inventory lists with purchase prices and a rule to research prices before setting them, the shop made a profit. Anthropic concluded that the structure and the tools did more than adding an AI chief executive.

## What domain knowledge looks like in a data and AI team

It is not a person who knows the business sitting next to the model. It is things the team writes down, tests and ships. The series shows six.

1. **KPI definitions that state their blind spot.** "Availability, from book stock: cannot see misplaced or stolen stock." The [KPI page](../kpis/) does this for eighteen KPIs.
2. **Tests about the world, not just the data.** Of Store 014's twelve data tests on keys, values and reconciliations, eleven pass, and the failure is about milk. Four tests that encode how a store works all fail; one says an item in stock does not go far longer than usual without a sale.
3. **An independent observation, joined.** A shelf audit, a case scan at receipt, the payment provider's final status. Each was in the data already, and nothing joined it.
4. **A model of how the data is observed.** Sales measure demand only while the shelf is stocked; a UPI timeout is a question, not a failure. A detector built on that idea finds the empty shelf in three open hours instead of 39.
5. **A domain layer for agents.** Definitions, rules and views took an AI assistant from one right answer in seven to seven in seven, on the same records.
6. **An answer key.** Every model in the series is scored against ground truth. In a real business, the answer keys are audits, counts, mystery shops and customer contacts, and it takes domain knowledge to decide which of them to trust.

## Two numbers for every KPI

The practice the series argues for is small. Every KPI that matters should carry two numbers: the value the systems report, and the value an independent observation gives. And one more: how long it takes to find the gap between them.

At Store 014 that took 39 open hours for the empty shelf, four days for the wrong pallet, and, for the customers charged twice, longer than any system ever looked: each one surfaced only when the customer complained. An agent acting on these KPIs would have acted on the reported value every time.

## Where to start

- List your five most important domain KPIs. For each, write down the record it is computed from and what that record cannot see.
- Find one independent observation for each, and join it.
- Before an agent acts on a KPI, give it the blind spot as a rule.
- Score your models against what happened, not against what was recorded.
