## Monday, 6:12 in the morning

The warehouse truck backs into Store 014's receiving bay at 6:12, twelve minutes into its 06:00–06:30 slot. It carries two pallets: 16 cases of 5 kg atta, 9 of 1 kg atta, 14 of rice and 5 of sugar.

The receiving clerk scans the two pallet labels. The handheld matches each label to the warehouse's shipping notice, and the store's books go up by exactly what the notice lists, including 64 bags of 5 kg atta. No discrepancies. The cases are put away before the store opens at seven.

By every record, the delivery is on time and in full. On the first pallet, the 16 bales on line 1 hold 1 kg packs, 160 of them. The 5 kg bags the notice promised are still in the warehouse, in the slot a scanner says they were picked from at three that morning.

## Five readings of one delivery

As in the retail story, nobody here is careless. Each person reads the record in front of them and reaches a reasonable conclusion.

**The DC shift supervisor** sees pick confirmations: every line of the order picked and confirmed by a scan, and the truck dispatched at 04:30 as scheduled. Later that week the atta bay throws up two count variances: 16 cases short in the 1 kg slot on Tuesday, and 16 cases over in the 5 kg slot on Thursday. Each is counted, corrected and closed.

**The transport manager** sees a trip log. TRK-07 left at 04:30 and arrived at 06:12, inside its slot. It made the slot on 23 of 25 mornings in June.

**The receiving clerk** sees two pallet labels that matched the notice. No discrepancies, booked before opening.

**The supply chain performance team** publishes the OTIF scorecard: 88% of June's 25 deliveries arrived in the slot and in full. The three that missed were late on 11 and 15 June and short on 11 and 13 June, none of them atta. The 22 June delivery counts as perfect.

**The store's stock controller** sees a count on Friday 26 June, ordered after customers complained about 5 kg atta. The books said 63 bags and the bay held none. The same count found 157 more 1 kg packs than the books showed. At cost, that is ₹14,364 of 5 kg atta gone and ₹7,772 of 1 kg from nowhere. Either the counting is sloppy, or 5 kg bags are walking out of the store.

## What actually happened

At 03:00 the picker went to slot A-07-03 for 16 cases of 5 kg atta, scanned the slot's label, and took 16 bales from A-07-04 next to it: the same brand's 1 kg packs, one slot along. The scan confirmed where the picker stood, not what the picker took.

Every record after that was written from the pick confirmation. The shipping notice listed 16 cases of 5 kg. The pallet label carried an SSCC, a serial number that says nothing about what is on the pallet; it points to the shipping notice that does. The goods receipt was booked by matching that label to that notice. Five records agreed with each other perfectly, and with the pallet not at all.

<DocumentsAgree />

The store's books now held 64 bags of 5 kg that did not exist, while 160 packs of 1 kg sat in the backroom off the books. The 5 kg shelf sold through the stock it had and ran out at 3:30 on Tuesday afternoon. That night and the next two, the reorder system saw 63 bags on the books against a reorder point of 40 or 41, and ordered nothing. The shelf stayed empty for 51.5 open hours, until Friday's count zeroed the books, the system ordered that night, and Saturday's truck refilled the bay.

<ShelfAfterPallet />

Customers wanted 72 bags of 5 kg during the gap and could not buy them, ₹19,080 at the shelf price. 29 of those became trips to another store, 16 a different pack size and 12 another brand. 9 shoppers came back later, and 6 bags were never bought.

## Four corrections, one mistake

The mis-pick left four records wrong, two in each building. Each was found and fixed on its own, days apart.

<Corrections />

The warehouse found its two errors as count variances, two days apart, and closed each one. The store found its two in a single count, after customers complained. Across the company the four corrections net to about ₹80, which is exactly why nobody chases them: the store's shrinkage report shows a loss of 5 kg atta, and the warehouse's shows two slots that needed recounting.

Read together, they carry a signature anyone who has worked in a warehouse will recognise: equal and opposite variances in neighbouring slots holding two sizes of the same product, mirrored at a store that received a truck from those slots. The question it raises is which truck those cases went on, and the pick confirmations answer it in a minute.

## This is the normal case

Mis-picks are not rare events that happen only to bad warehouses. They are a rate, and good warehouses measure it.

- WERC's annual DC Measures benchmark puts best-in-class order-picking accuracy at 99.68% or better. Even at that rate about one in 300 still goes wrong, and a store that takes a truck almost every morning will see it happen.
- Receiving against a shipping notice is the design, not a shortcut. Under GS1's standards the shipper sends a despatch advice that identifies every pallet by its SSCC, so the receiver can scan a label instead of counting cases. In a GS1 case study, Carrefour Belgium describes scanning the SSCC of every incoming pallet.
- Checking is expensive, so it is sampled. In Auburn University's 2017 study of shipping notices from suppliers, retail warehouses physically checked an average of 57% of orders, and only about one retailer in ten inspected every unit of every delivery. The median accuracy those checks found was 92%, against the retailers' median goal of 98%.

A store receiving from its own company's warehouse has even less reason to doubt the notice. In this world the warehouse shipped 70 order lines to Store 014 in June and got one wrong. That is worse than a best-in-class warehouse manages, and it was enough.

## One name, four numbers

OTIF, on time and in full, sounds like one measure. Ask how the warehouse did for Store 014 in June and you can get four honest answers.

<OtifFourWays />

The warehouse measures its own dispatch: the truck left on schedule and every line went out as ordered, so only the two short shipments count against it. The scorecard measures arrival in the slot, and fullness by the store's goods receipt, either per order line or per delivery. Only the last measure looks at what was actually on the pallets, and nobody computes it, because nobody records it. The 22 June delivery passes the first three and fails the fourth.

Real programmes move their definitions too. When Walmart began fining suppliers 3% of the value of late or missing goods in 2017, early deliveries were fined as well. In 2019 it required full truckloads to arrive within a two-day window 87% of the time, and split the measure in two so suppliers would focus on delivering in full; from September 2020 the target was 98%. A number called OTIF can only be compared with another number called OTIF when the window and the record behind "in full" are the same.

## What would have helped

The demo lets you try three changes from 1 June, with the same customers and the same trucks.

- **Scan every case at pick.** The scanner reads the barcode on the bale instead of the slot's label, and rejects a 1 kg bale on a 5 kg line at 03:00. The right cases go on the pallet, every record downstream is true, and the scorecard and the shelf agree at 88%. The cost is one more scan per case, on every line, every night.
- **Weigh every pallet at dispatch.** With 1 kg bales on the 5 kg line, the first pallet weighs about 564 kg against the 724 kg its notice implies, 22% light. The line is re-picked, and the truck leaves at 05:00 instead of 04:30 and reaches the store at 06:42, outside its slot. Only one customer all week misses a 5 kg bag, and the scorecard falls to 84%, because the delivery that kept the shelf full arrived late.
- **Scan every case at the store.** The clerk finds 1 kg bales on line 1, books 160 packs of 1 kg instead of 64 bags of 5 kg, and raises a claim. The warehouse corrects both slots that morning, the reorder system orders 5 kg that night, and no customer finds the 5 kg shelf empty. The scorecard falls to 84% here too, because it now records what arrived.

Two of the three fixes make the headline number worse. That may be the most useful thing a logistics metric can teach: a measure that reads only the sender's records gets better when you stop checking, and worse when you start.

## How to learn this domain

A rough plan for a few evenings a week over about two months:

1. **How a warehouse works.** Read Bartholdi and Hackman's *Warehouse & Distribution Science*, free to download from the authors, for receiving, slotting and order picking, and Gwynne Richards's *Warehouse Management* for day-to-day operations. If you can, walk a warehouse from the receiving dock to dispatch.
2. **Supply chain fundamentals.** MIT's Supply Chain Fundamentals (CTL.SC1x) covers demand, inventory and transport, with the maths and the industry practice.
3. **Documents and identifiers.** Learn the GS1 logistic label, the SSCC and the despatch advice, and how a receiver matches them. In India, add the GST documents that move with goods: an e-way bill is required before moving most consignments worth more than ₹50,000, including movements that are not sales, such as transfers between a company's own sites (rule 138 of the CGST Rules; states can set a different limit for movement within the state).
4. **Metrics.** OTIF, case fill, pick accuracy and dock-to-stock time. Read how Walmart's OTIF programme changed, and look at WERC's DC Measures for how warehouses benchmark themselves.
5. **Practice.** [Download June's shipping records](../data/#logistics-records), compute OTIF from them per delivery and per order line, then find the 22 June line without opening the answer key.
