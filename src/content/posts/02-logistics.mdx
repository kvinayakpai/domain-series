Every number in this post comes from the same synthetic world as the [retail post](../retail/): one fictional supermarket in Jayanagar, Bengaluru, the warehouse in Hoskote that supplies it, and the dairy route that delivers its milk, simulated hour by hour through June 2026. The store, the warehouse and their people are invented. Being synthetic, the world has an answer key: it knows what was really on every pallet, so every reading of the data below can be scored.

This post makes the argument for data and AI teams. Its [technical companion](./technical/) has the tests in SQL, the trace from a stock correction to a pallet line, and a notebook that runs on the shipping records. The [demo](../demo/#logistics) lets you change how the warehouse checks its picks and how the store receives, and watch what follows.

## Monday, 6:12 in the morning

The warehouse truck backs into Store 014's receiving bay at 6:12, twelve minutes into its 06:00–06:30 slot. It carries two pallets: 16 cases of 5 kg atta, 9 of 1 kg atta, 14 of rice and 5 of sugar.

The receiving clerk scans the two pallet labels. The handheld matches each label to the warehouse's shipping notice, and the store's books go up by exactly what the notice lists, including 64 bags of 5 kg atta. No discrepancies. The cases are put away before the store opens at seven.

By every record, the delivery is on time and in full. On the first pallet, the 16 bales on line 1 hold 1 kg packs, 160 of them. The 5 kg bags the notice promised are still in the warehouse, in the slot a scanner says they were picked from at three that morning.

## Five readings of one delivery

As in the retail story, nobody here is careless. Each data product reads the records in front of it and reaches a reasonable conclusion.

**The OTIF dashboard** reads the scorecard: 88% of June’s 25 deliveries arrived in the slot and in full. The three that missed were late on 11 and 15 June and short on 11 and 13 June, none of them atta. The 22 June delivery counts as perfect. The warehouse’s own report says 92%, and its trip log says TRK-07 left at 04:30 and arrived at 06:12; the truck made the slot on 23 of 25 mornings in June.

**The data tests** pass, all ten: keys unique, every notice line confirmed by a pick scan, every pallet in the pallet table, every line received as the notice listed it. The shipping notice, the pick confirmations, the pallets and the goods receipts join without a single mismatch.

**The inventory reports** show four corrections in the atta bay in four days, two in each building. The warehouse counted 16 cases short in its 1 kg slot on Tuesday and 16 cases over in its 5 kg slot on Thursday, and closed each. Across the company the corrections net to about ₹80.

**The store’s loss report** shows a count on Friday 26 June, ordered after customers complained about 5 kg atta. The books said 63 bags and the bay held none. The same count found 157 more 1 kg packs than the books showed. At cost, that is ₹14,364 of 5 kg atta gone and ₹7,772 of 1 kg from nowhere. Either the counting is sloppy, or 5 kg bags are walking out of the store.

**An AI assistant, given the shipping schema,** is asked at the supplier review on Monday 29 June whether the 22 June delivery was on time and in full. Yes, it says: it arrived at 06:12, inside its slot, and all four lines were booked as the shipping notice listed them, with no discrepancies. Asked whether the missing 5 kg atta is theft, it says possibly, and suggests referring it to loss prevention.

## What actually happened

At 03:00 the picker went to slot A-07-03 for 16 cases of 5 kg atta, scanned the slot's label, and took 16 bales from A-07-04 next to it: the same brand's 1 kg packs, one slot along. The scan confirmed where the picker stood, not what the picker took.

Every record after that was written from the pick confirmation. The shipping notice listed 16 cases of 5 kg. The pallet label carried an SSCC, a serial number that says nothing about what is on the pallet; it points to the shipping notice that does. The goods receipt was booked by matching that label to that notice. Five records agreed with each other perfectly, and with the pallet not at all.

<DocumentsAgree />

The store's books now held 64 bags of 5 kg that did not exist, while 160 packs of 1 kg sat in the backroom off the books. The 5 kg shelf sold through the stock it had and ran out at 3:30 on Tuesday afternoon. That night and the next two, the reorder system saw 63 bags on the books against a reorder point of 40 or 41, and ordered nothing. The shelf stayed empty for 51.5 open hours, until Friday's count zeroed the books, the system ordered that night, and Saturday's truck refilled the bay.

<ShelfAfterPallet />

Customers wanted 72 bags of 5 kg during the gap and could not buy them, ₹19,080 at the shelf price. 29 of those became trips to another store, 16 a different pack size and 12 another brand. 9 shoppers came back later, and 6 bags were never bought.

## Why the data could not say so

Every record about the delivery was written from one event, the pick confirmation at 03:00. The shipping notice copied it, the pallet label pointed to the notice, the goods receipt copied the notice, and the scorecard read the receipt. Data tests check that records agree with each other, and five records written from one source always agree.

"In full" is a fact about the cases on a pallet, and no record in June observed a case. Nobody weighed a pallet: the pallet table's weight column is the weight the notice implies, computed from the notice. Nobody scanned a case at the store. The only independent observations came later, in two buildings, as count corrections that each team closed on its own.

## Four corrections, one mistake

The mis-pick left four records wrong, two in each building. Each was found and fixed on its own, days apart.

<Corrections />

The warehouse found its two errors as count variances, two days apart, and closed each one. The store found its two in a single count, after customers complained. Across the company the four corrections net to about ₹80, which is exactly why nobody chases them: the store's shrinkage report shows a loss of 5 kg atta, and the warehouse's shows two slots that needed recounting.

Read together, they carry a signature anyone who has worked in a warehouse will recognise: equal and opposite variances in neighbouring slots holding two sizes of the same product, mirrored at a store that received a truck from those slots. The question it raises is which truck those cases went on, and the pick confirmations answer it in a minute.

## What domain knowledge changes in the data work

Each change below uses only the records, and each is scored against the answer key.

**Ask which records are independent.** A test that asks whether anything other than the pick checks what is on each pallet, a scale weight or a case scan, fails for all 30 of June's pallets. That one test tells a data team which part of its OTIF number rests on a copy: all of "in full".

<LogisticsTests />

**Pair corrections before you explain them.** Two corrections mirror each other when they are in one product family, have opposite signs, are within 15% of each other in cases and fall within a week: neighbouring slots at the warehouse, two items at one store count, or the same item in both buildings. June has five such pairs. Four join the four atta corrections, which net to ₹80 at cost against a gross of ₹44,648. The fifth is toned and full-cream milk at the month-end count, the till's scan errors from the retail post.

<MirrorPairs />

**Trace the pair to the pick.** The warehouse's pair says 16 cases left the 1 kg slot as 5 kg. A query for 16-case picks of 5 kg from slot A-07-03 in the week before the first variance returns one row: line 1 of the 22 June shipment, picked at 03:00 and confirmed by a slot-label scan. The answer key confirms that line held 160 packs of 1 kg. The warehouse's pair was complete at 10:00 on Thursday 25 June, three days after the truck and a day before the store's count. With the trace, the store could have corrected its books and reordered that night, a day sooner.

**Reconcile the KPI with an observation.** A receipt booked by a label scan is a copy of the notice; a count is an observation. Flag the latest receipt before a count when the count takes off half or more of what it booked, and one line in June fails: 64 bags of 5 kg booked on 22 June, of which the 26 June count took off 63. Take that delivery out of the scorecard and OTIF from the records falls from 88% to 84%, what the answer key says reached the shelf.

**Model how the shelf data is observed.** The retail post's gap detector, on the till lines, flags 5 kg atta at 20:00 on Tuesday 23 June, with 63 bags on the books. Filling the hours it flagged, and the hours the books show no stock, with the sales it expected puts the gap's cost at about 82 bags; the answer key says 72.

**Give the assistant a domain layer.** Ask the same assistant the same seven questions, now given definitions ("an SSCC identifies a pallet, not what is on it"; "a goods receipt booked by label scan is a copy of the notice"), the pick confirmations, pallets and warehouse adjustments, and five views built from them, the mirror pairs among them. It answers all seven. Given the schema alone, it answered one.

## This is the normal case

Mis-picks are not rare events that happen only to bad warehouses. They are a rate, and good warehouses measure it.

- WERC's annual DC Measures benchmark puts best-in-class order-picking accuracy at 99.68% or better. Even at that rate about one in 300 still goes wrong, and a store that takes a truck almost every morning will see it happen.
- Receiving against a shipping notice is the design, not a shortcut. Under GS1's standards the shipper sends a despatch advice that identifies every pallet by its SSCC, so the receiver can scan a label instead of counting cases. In a GS1 case study, Carrefour Belgium describes scanning the SSCC of every incoming pallet.
- Checking is expensive, so it is sampled. In Auburn University's 2017 study of shipping notices from suppliers, retail warehouses physically checked an average of 57% of orders, and only about one retailer in ten inspected every unit of every delivery. The median accuracy those checks found was 92%, against the retailers' median goal of 98%.

A store receiving from its own company's warehouse has even less reason to doubt the notice. In this world the warehouse shipped 70 order lines to Store 014 in June and got one wrong. That is worse than a best-in-class warehouse manages, and it was enough.

## One name, four numbers

OTIF, on time and in full, sounds like one measure. Ask how the warehouse did for Store 014 in June and you can get four honest answers.

<OtifFourWays />

The warehouse measures its own dispatch: the truck left on schedule and every line went out as ordered, so only the two short shipments count against it. The scorecard measures arrival in the slot, and fullness by the store's goods receipt, either per order line or per delivery. Only the last measure looks at what was actually on the pallets, and nobody computes it, because nobody records it. The 22 June delivery passes the first three and fails the fourth. Reconciled with the counts, the records give 84% too, but only after a count has found the gap.

Real programmes move their definitions too. When Walmart began fining suppliers 3% of the value of late or missing goods in 2017, early deliveries were fined as well. In 2019 it required full truckloads to arrive within a two-day window 87% of the time, and split the measure in two so suppliers would focus on delivering in full; from September 2020 the target was 98%. A number called OTIF can only be compared with another number called OTIF when the window and the record behind "in full" are the same.

## What would have helped

The demo lets you try three changes from 1 June, with the same customers and the same trucks.

- **Scan every case at pick.** The scanner reads the barcode on the bale instead of the slot's label, and rejects a 1 kg bale on a 5 kg line at 03:00. The right cases go on the pallet, every record downstream is true, and the scorecard and the shelf agree at 88%. The cost is one more scan per case, on every line, every night.
- **Weigh every pallet at dispatch.** With 1 kg bales on the 5 kg line, the first pallet weighs about 564 kg against the 724 kg its notice implies, 22% light. The line is re-picked, and the truck leaves at 05:00 instead of 04:30 and reaches the store at 06:42, outside its slot. Only one customer all week misses a 5 kg bag, and the scorecard falls to 84%, because the delivery that kept the shelf full arrived late.
- **Scan every case at the store.** The clerk finds 1 kg bales on line 1, books 160 packs of 1 kg instead of 64 bags of 5 kg, and raises a claim. The warehouse corrects both slots that morning, the reorder system orders 5 kg that night, and no customer finds the 5 kg shelf empty. The scorecard falls to 84% here too, because it now records what arrived.

Two of the three fixes make the headline number worse. That may be the most useful thing a logistics metric can teach: a measure that reads only the sender's records gets better when you stop checking, and worse when you start.

## For data and AI leaders: five questions to ask your team

1. Which of our records were written from the same event, and which observe the goods independently?
2. Do we net our stock corrections, or pair them?
3. When a KPI says "in full", which record says so, and could anything have contradicted it?
4. Can our data trace a variance back to the order line, the pallet and the pick that caused it?
5. What does our AI assistant know about how a warehouse works, and how do we test that?
