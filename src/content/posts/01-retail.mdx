This series looks at domain knowledge from the side of the people who build on a business's data: the pipelines, the metrics, the models and, now, the AI assistants. Each post takes one failure that only domain knowledge explains, shows what each data product made of it, and measures what changes when the domain is built in.

The numbers come from a synthetic world built for the series: one fictional supermarket in Jayanagar, Bengaluru, its warehouse and its dairy route, simulated hour by hour through June 2026 and calibrated against published studies of real stores. Being synthetic, it has what real data never has: ground truth. Every model here is scored against it.

This post makes the argument. Its [technical companion](./technical/) is for the people who will build the fix: the tests in SQL, the models in Python, and a notebook that runs on the store's records.

## Monday, 9 a.m.: the weekly review

It is Monday 15 June, and Store 014's weekly trading review opens on the sales dashboard. Whole wheat atta in 5 kg bags is down 59% on the week: 88 bags against 217. The overnight pipeline ran green. The demand forecast has eased, and the replenishment plan follows it.

Someone asks the store's AI assistant why sales fell. It queries the warehouse and answers in seconds:

## Five readings of one week

Nobody here is careless. Each data product reads its inputs correctly.

**The pipeline.** Twelve data tests run every night: keys unique, values present, every barcode in the product master, daily sales reconciled to the till lines, and book stock reconciled to its own ledger, each night's close equal to the last close plus receipts, minus sales and write-offs, plus count adjustments. Eleven pass. The twelfth flags full-cream milk below zero on six days, a familiar symptom of scan errors. Nothing flags 5 kg atta.

**The dashboard.** June's on-shelf availability for 5 kg atta, computed from book stock, is 97%. Sales are down 59%.

**The forecast.** The store forecasts each item as the 28-day mean of recorded sales: 24.3 bags a day on 9 June, 23.6 by 14 June, 20.2 by the end of the month. Scored against what the tills recorded, it looks 13% too high over June, so the obvious tuning would lower it further.

**The mix analysis.** 5 kg's share of atta units fell from 40% to 25% in a week. Read as a preference, that says give the 1 kg pack more shelf.

**The AI assistant.** Asked seven questions about the first half of June, it gets one right: the one about what the tills scanned.

## What actually happened

On Tuesday 9 June at 06:17, fourteen cases of 5 kg atta from the morning truck were put away behind the rice stack, in the wrong bay. On Wednesday at 15:48 the shelf ran out. The books said 56 bags, above the reorder point of 40, so nothing was ordered. The shelf stood empty for 39 open hours while the system showed stock, until staff restacking the rice found the cases on Saturday morning. Shoppers asked for 71 bags they could not find.

Demand did ease that week, after the first week of the month, when households stock up: shoppers came for 224 bags in the first week of June and 148 in the second. That explains part of the drop in sales. The rest was a shelf with nothing on it. Across the month, demand was flat: 24.7 bags a day in May, 24.9 in June.

## Why the data could not say so

Three traps sit under this week, and anyone who has built on transaction data has stepped in at least one.

**Sales are censored demand.** A till records a sale, not a shopper. When the shelf is empty, sales measure the shelf. Every model trained on sales inherits the gap: forecasts, price elasticities, mix shares, recommendations. Economists who tracked stock in 54 vending machines found that ignoring stock-outs can bias demand estimates and understate what stock-outs cost (Conlon and Mortimer, 2013).

**Book stock is a belief, not an observation.** It is computed: the last count plus every receipt, minus every sale and recorded write-off. That is why every reconciliation test passes. It agreed with every transaction and was wrong about the shelf for three days. A data test is an assertion about your data; none of them looks at the shelf. In a study of nearly 370,000 inventory records from 37 stores of one retailer, 65% were wrong (DeHoratius and Raman, 2008).

**Your evaluation data is censored too.** Scored against recorded sales, the store's forecast for 5 kg atta looks 13% too high over June. Scored against the shoppers who came for it, it is 8% too low. The holdout set has the same hole as the training set, so the better a model fits it, the worse the model gets.

None of this is rare. A worldwide study of grocery out-of-stocks put the average rate at 8.3% and traced most of them to the store itself (FMI, GMA and CIES, 2002). The world behind this post is calibrated to those figures: across its seven warehouse items, shelves stood empty for 5% of open hours in June.

## What domain knowledge changes in the data work

Each of the five changes below uses only the store's own records, and each is scored against the answer key.

**Model how the data is observed.** A gap detector learns each item's usual hourly sales from May's till lines. It adds up the sales it expects over every run of open hours with no sales while the books show stock, and raises an alert once a zero that long is less likely than 1 in 1,000. On June's records it flags 5 kg atta at 19:00 on Wednesday, three open hours after the shelf emptied. Across the seven warehouse items it raises 10 alerts; 6 fall in real gaps, and it finds 6 of the 10 gaps that lasted three open hours or more. Rerun June with someone checking the shelf within an hour of each alert, and 5 kg atta loses 35 bags instead of 157.

<DetectorTable />

The threshold is a business decision. One in a hundred finds more gaps and sends staff to more full shelves; one in ten thousand rarely bothers them and finds gaps later. The nightly rule a BI team might ship first, sales under a quarter of the forecast for a day while the books show stock, never cried wolf in June, but it found 2 of the 10 gaps, a median 14 open hours in.

**Unconstrain demand before you train.** Fill every censored hour with the sales the detector expected (the runs it flagged, and the hours the books show no stock), and the 28-day forecast holds: 25.6 bags a day at the end of June instead of 20.2, against the 24.9 that shoppers came for. Its bias over June falls from −8% to −1%, and the order-up-to level the reorder rule would set rises from 84 bags to 93.

**Join the observation you already have.** The store audits its shelves at 11:00 every Tuesday and Friday. On Friday 12 June the audit found the 5 kg bay empty while the books showed 55. The audit table was in the warehouse all along; no pipeline, dashboard or model joined it.

**Give the assistant a domain layer.** Ask the same seven questions of the same assistant, now given definitions ("sales measure demand only while the item is on the shelf"; "book stock is a belief, not an observation"), the till lines, the shelf audits, and four views built from them: hourly sales, the hourly book, the censored hours and a demand estimate. It answers all seven.

Published work points the same way. On the BIRD text-to-SQL benchmark, linking questions to what the data actually holds is one of the hard parts, and the best model of 2023 reached 40% execution accuracy against 93% for people (Li and others, 2023). On enterprise questions over an insurance database, GPT-4 answered 16% correctly from the SQL schema and 54% from a knowledge graph of the same data (Sequeda, Allemang and Jacob, 2023). The model matters less than what it is given about the domain. The assistant here is scripted so the run can be repeated; the companion shows every query, and the notebook prints a prompt to try on your own model.

**Or capture the missing event.** If every case is scanned into a numbered backroom bin at put-away, the record knows where the stock is, and 5 kg atta loses 1 bag in June instead of 157. Sometimes the best model is a barcode scan.

## For data and AI leaders: five questions to ask your team

1. Which of our tables are observations, and which are beliefs computed from other tables?
2. Where does our data stop recording what we are trying to measure?
3. Which independent observation do we have, and does anything join to it?
4. What do we score our models against, and does it share their blind spot?
5. What does our AI assistant know about how the business works, and how do we test that?
