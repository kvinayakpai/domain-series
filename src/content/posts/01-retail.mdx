Every number in this post comes from a synthetic world built for this series: one fictional supermarket in Jayanagar, Bengaluru, the warehouse that supplies it and the dairy route that delivers its milk, simulated hour by hour through June 2026. The store and its people are invented. How the numbers behave is calibrated against published studies of real stores, and the [demo](../demo/#retail) lets you change what the store does and watch what follows.

## Thursday, 7:40 in the evening

It is the evening rush at Store 014. The bay for 5 kg bags of whole wheat atta is empty, and has been since 3:48 on Wednesday afternoon.

A customer asks for it. A staff member checks the handheld: 55 bags on hand. They look in the backroom, find nothing, and tell the customer it must be on the next truck.

No truck is coming. Every night at ten, the store's reorder system compares the stock on its books with a reorder point. On Wednesday, Thursday and Friday nights it saw 55 or 56 bags against a reorder point of 39 or 40, and ordered nothing. The shelf stays empty until Saturday morning.

## Five readings of one shelf

Nobody in this story is careless. Four people and one system each read the record in front of them and reach a reasonable conclusion. Each conclusion is wrong.

**The store manager** sees an empty bay and a handheld that says otherwise. The stock must be in transit.

**The reorder system** sees 55 bags on the books and a reorder point of 40. In stock, no reorder needed, three nights running.

**The demand planner** sees the weekly sales report. The store sold 217 bags in the first week of June and 88 in the second, down 59%. The forecast, a 28-day average of recorded sales, slides from 24.3 bags a day before the gap to 20.2 by the end of June. Demand for 5 kg looks like it is falling and getting erratic, so the order should come down.

**The category manager** sees the pack mix. The 5 kg pack was 40% of atta units sold in the first week and 25% in the second. Shoppers seem to be moving to other pack sizes, so perhaps 5 kg should give some of its shelf to 1 kg.

**The atta supplier's sales rep** sees orders from Store 014 fall from 172 bags to 112 in a week. A competitor must be taking the space.

## What actually happened

On Tuesday 9 June at 06:17, the warehouse truck delivered 14 cases of 5 kg atta, 56 bags. The receiving clerk scanned the pallet label, the books went up by 56, and the cases were put away behind the rice stack, in the wrong bay.

The shelf sold through what it had and ran out at 3:48 on Wednesday afternoon. From then on, every bag the system counted was sitting behind the rice. The books still said 55 or 56, so the reorder system had no reason to act, and the shelf stayed empty for 39 open hours, until staff restacking the rice found the cases at ten on Saturday morning.

<BookVsShelf />

Customers wanted about 157 bags that week. The tills recorded 88. Of the 71 bags customers wanted during the gap and could not buy, 26 became sales of another brand and 13 a different pack size. 19 became trips to another store, 8 shoppers came back later, and 5 bags were never bought at all.

Demand never fell. Customers wanted 24.7 bags a day on average in May and 24.9 in June. What fell was the shelf.

## This is the normal case

It would be comforting to call this a freak event. The research says otherwise.

- A worldwide study of grocery out-of-stocks for FMI, GMA and CIES put the average out-of-stock rate at 8.3%. It traced about 47% of out-of-stocks to store ordering and forecasting and 25% to shelf management. Most empty shelves start inside the store, not at the warehouse.
- DeHoratius and Raman examined nearly 370,000 inventory records from 37 stores of one retailer and found 65% of them inaccurate.
- Kang and Gershwin showed how small, unrecorded losses can stall replenishment. When the records show stock that is not there, automatic reordering waits for sales that cannot happen until someone corrects the record.

The world behind this post is calibrated to those figures. Across the store's seven warehouse items, shelves stood empty for 5% of open hours in June, 74% of those hours started in the store, and 8 of 11 items had wrong records at the month-end count.

## One name, four numbers

Ask how available 5 kg atta was at Store 014 in June and you can get four honest answers.

<FourWays />

The system counts hours with stock on the books, so the cases behind the rice count as available. The shelf audit visits twice a week at 11:00; it happened to catch the empty bay on Friday 12 June and again on 26 June, so two of eight visits found it empty. The hourly view counts open hours with a bag on the shelf. The customer view weights each hour by how many people wanted a bag. None of these is wrong. A dashboard that shows one without saying which one it is, is.

The forecast has the same problem in a quieter form. It learns from recorded sales, and recorded sales are capped by what was on the shelf. Against recorded sales, the forecast was off by 54% in June. Against what customers actually wanted, it was off by 33%. The demand planner sees a volatile item, but the truth is a steady item with a broken shelf. Filling the empty-shelf hours with expected sales would have left the forecast at 25.7 bags a day at the end of June, not 20.2.

## Same store, different item: milk

Milk breaks the pattern, which is the point. It never passes through the warehouse. The dairy's truck drops a standing order at 05:30. Pouch milk is short-lived: Amul's Taaza, for example, is good for 48 hours from packing, so in practice a pouch can be sold on the day it arrives and the next. There is no reorder point to freeze. The risk runs the other way: order too much and pouches expire, order too little and the shelf is empty by evening.

<MilkTable />

In this world we assume each pouch of toned milk earns the store ₹1.30 and costs it ₹24.70 when it expires. At today's standing order, 291 pouches expired in June, eating ₹7,188 of the ₹11,309 the store made on milk. Cut the order by 5% and what milk earns more than doubles, to ₹10,810, while the pouches customers wanted and could not find rise from 119 to 269, and the milk shelf runs dry on 6 evenings in June instead of 2.

Which order is right depends on something no margin report shows: how many of those shoppers came in for milk and would have bought a basket. The category view and the store manager's view disagree, and both are reading their numbers correctly.

## What would have helped

The demo lets you try two changes the store could make from 1 June, with exactly the same customers.

- **A low-sales alert.** It flags an item selling under a quarter of its forecast while the books show stock, and sends someone to count it the next morning. With it, the 5 kg shelf's empty hours in June fall from 97 to 59, and bags customers could not buy fall from 157 to 90. It still waits a day, and a single mis-scanned pack can hide an empty shelf from it.
- **Scanning every case into a numbered backroom bin.** Cases are scanned into a bin at put-away, and refill rounds run on a fixed schedule. Nothing goes missing behind the rice. The second incident of the month, on 22 June, never reaches the shelf either: the put-away scan catches a pallet line labelled 5 kg that holds 1 kg packs. Unmet demand for the month falls to a single bag.

Detection helps, and prevention helps more. Telling which one a given failure needs is what domain knowledge is for.

The signal is a pattern across tables. Book stock stops moving at the same moment sales stop, with no delivery, write-off or count in between. The till lines show no 5 kg sales; the Friday audit records an empty bay; the book stays at 55. Each table is consistent on its own, and the story only appears when you join them. Knowing which join to make comes from knowing how a store runs.

The world uses real retail identifiers: GTIN-13 product codes, SSCC pallet labels and GLN location codes. All of them sit under GS1 prefix 29, which GS1 sets aside for restricted circulation within a company or a region, so none of them can be mistaken for a globally issued product barcode.

## How to learn this domain

A rough plan for a few evenings a week over about two months:

1. **How stores actually run.** Read *Execution: The Missing Link in Retail Operations* by Raman, DeHoratius and Ton, and the abstract of DeHoratius and Raman's study of record accuracy. Then walk a supermarket and note where stock lives: shelf, backroom, receiving bay.
2. **Inventory policy.** Reorder points, min/max rules, safety stock and service levels; any operations-management textbook covers them. Then read about stock loss stalling replenishment, starting with Yun Kang's MIT thesis.
3. **Forecasting retail demand.** Work through Hyndman and Athanasopoulos's *Forecasting: Principles and Practice*, free online, then try the M5 competition's Walmart unit-sales data on Kaggle. Pay attention to zero-sales days: which mean no demand, and which mean an empty shelf?
4. **Store data.** Learn the tables (till lines, book stock, receipts, counts, shelf audits) and the identifiers that join them: GTIN, GLN and SSCC.
5. **Practice.** [Download June's records from Store 014](../data/) and try the four exercises before opening the answer key.
