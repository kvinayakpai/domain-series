What does availability mean to you? That the system shows stock, or that a shopper can actually pick the product up? [On-shelf availability](https://www.ecr-community.org/osa/) asks the second question. A product counts as available only if it is in the place the shopper would look, at the time the shopper comes. An empty shelf sends shoppers away. As per [a worldwide study of grocery out-of-stocks](https://www.fmi.org/newsroom/news-archive/view/2002/10/14/new-industry-study-explores-root-causes-of-global-out-of-stock-dilemma), 31% of shoppers go to another store when an item is missing, and 9% buy nothing at all.

## What the dashboard said

Store 014 is the fictional supermarket in Bengaluru that I simulated, hour by hour, through June 2026. Its dashboard said 5 kg bags of [atta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atta_(flour)), the wheat flour used in India for flatbreads such as rotis, were available in 97% of the store's open hours.

Because I built the store, I know where every bag was. A bag was on the shelf in 77% of those hours.

## What happened

On Tuesday 9 June, fourteen cases of 5 kg atta from the morning truck were put away in the wrong bay, behind the rice. The shelf ran out at 15:48 the next day, while the system still showed 56 bags. Staff found the cases on Saturday morning, after the shelf had stood empty for 39 open hours, and in that time shoppers came for 71 bags that were not there.

It happened again later in the month. A pallet labelled 5 kg turned out to hold 1 kg packs, and the 5 kg shelf stood empty for another 51.5 open hours.

**The dashboard said 97%, and the shelf said 77%.**

## Why the number missed it

The dashboard never looks at the shelf. It counts an hour as available if the system shows at least one bag in stock. That stock figure is called book stock, and it is the last count plus deliveries, minus sales and write-offs. Nothing in that sum looks at the shelf, so the bags behind the rice stayed on the books, and every hour they sat there counted as available.

I believe this is common in real stores. In [a study of nearly 370,000 inventory records from 37 stores](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v54y2008i4p627-641.html), 65% were wrong.

## How to catch it

Store 014 already had two ways to see the gap.

The first is to look. Staff check the shelves at 11:00 every Tuesday and Friday, and on Friday 12 June the check found the 5 kg bay empty while the system showed 55 bags. No report joined that check to the dashboard.

The second is to listen to the tills. Shoppers come for about 25 bags of 5 kg atta a day, so a long run with no sales while the system shows stock usually means the shelf is empty. A simple detector built on that idea flags the gap three open hours after the shelf emptied, instead of 39, and [researchers have built similar systems](https://uniwacris.uniwa.gr/handle/3000/2145) that spot empty shelves from sales and other store data.

## If an AI agent reads it

The agents in this series are scripted. A few fixed rules stand in for a language model, so every run can be repeated and scored.

A replenishment agent that read availability from book stock saw 5 kg atta 100% available but selling less than half its forecast, decided demand had dropped, and cut the forecast. It never asked anyone to check the shelf.

The same agent with one more rule, that in stock means on the shelf, asked for about six shelf checks a month. Across forty simulated Junes, 5 kg atta lost 105 bags on average instead of 214, without holding more stock.

## Try it on your own data

Pick your ten best-selling products. For each, find the last time a person looked at the shelf, and compare what they saw with what the system said at that hour. If no record shows anyone looking, that is your first finding.

So in summary, availability is a statement about the shelf, and book stock cannot make that statement.
