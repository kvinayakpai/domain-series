In my previous three blogs I linked the eighteen KPIs of this series to three incidents at Store 014: bags of 5 kg atta in the wrong bay, a pallet line of the wrong pack size, and customers charged twice when the tills gave up on UPI payments. In this last linking blog I want to put all of it on one map.

The map asks two questions any data team can ask of its own records. Which records are copies of each other, and which ones look at the world?

## Join the records two at a time

Let me explain what I did. I took every pair of June's records that share a key, twelve joins in all, and counted how often each pair agrees.

<JoinTable />

Eight of the twelve agree on every row. The shipping notice matches the pick confirmations on all 70 lines, the book stock matches its own ledger on all 297 item-days, and all 18,384 UPI payments the tills approved are successes in the provider's report. They agree because one record was written from the other. A test on such a join can only pass.

The other four sometimes disagree, and each of June's failures sits on one of them. The book stock disagrees with 21 of 25 stock counts and with two of 88 shelf checks. One goods receipt in ten did not survive the next count. And 32 of 48 timeouts disagree with the provider's report, because the till said failed and the customer's bank had sent the money.

**The joins that always agree compare a record with its own copy. The failures hide in the joins that sometimes disagree.**

## Who saw what

On each of those four joins, one side looks at the world: someone counting, someone looking at the shelf, the customer's bank. Now draw the records as a map: what writes each one, what it looks at, and which KPI reads it.

<RecordMap />

Three things stand out. The headline KPIs read copies. On-shelf availability reads the book stock, OTIF reads the goods receipts, and payment success reads the till. The records that look at the world sit at the bottom, and no headline KPI reads them. Nothing in June looked at what was on a pallet: none of the 30 pallets was weighed or case-scanned.

[W3C's PROV standard](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-o/) already has names for most of this map: a record *was generated by* an activity, and *was derived from* another record. The edges to the world, which record looks at which thing, are the part only someone who knows the domain can draw. I believe they are also the part an AI agent needs most.

## Hours by joining, days by waiting

Joining two of the store's own records finds each failure within hours. The empty atta shelf shows three open hours after it emptied. The wrong pallet line shows four and a half hours after the 5 kg shelf emptied. Each double charge shows a median 14 hours after the debit.

Waiting took days. Staff found the atta bags 66 hours after the shelf emptied, the manager's count found the missing 5 kg bags 66 hours after, and complaints took a median 48 hours, while three customers never complained at all.

## Give the agent the map

In the practitioner post that follows, a scripted assistant answers 22 questions about Store 014. Given only the schema, it gets 3 right. Given each domain's definitions and rules, it gets 21, and given the map, all 22.

Outside my simulation the evidence points the same way. In one benchmark, [GPT-4 answered 16% of enterprise questions correctly from the SQL tables, and 54% when the same data came as a knowledge graph of the business](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07509). [Checking each generated query against the business's ontology raised accuracy to 72%](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.11706).

## Five questions for data and AI leaders

1. For each KPI an agent acts on, which record does it read, and is that record a copy?
2. Which of our joins have never disagreed, and do we know why?
3. What in our business does no record look at?
4. When two departments' records disagree, who joins them, and how soon?
5. Can our agents read the map: what each record sees, and what can contradict it?

So in summary, my learning from eighteen KPIs and three incidents is simple. Check which of your records are copies of each other, and give your agent a map of who saw what. In the agentic era, I believe that map is the domain knowledge your data and AI teams should be building.
