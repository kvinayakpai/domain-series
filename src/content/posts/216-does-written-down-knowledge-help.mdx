## What is a knowledge layer?

A knowledge layer is the business, written down for an AI: what its words mean, what each record can and cannot see, how each KPI is worked out, and the rules the work must follow. It sits between the records and the AI. A good AI reads it before it reads the tables.

Earlier in this season we called the heart of it an ontology – the things a business deals in, how they connect and which rules must hold. The knowledge layer is the ontology plus everything that ties it to the real records and the real KPIs.

## What goes into it, and what does the AI do with each part?

For Store 014, the pretend supermarket of this series, the knowledge layer is nearly a thousand words of plain English. It has five parts.

1. **Words.** A glossary: OSA means on-shelf availability, and an RRN is the number UPI gives each payment. *The AI uses it to read a question the way the business means it.*
2. **Records.** A line on each table – what it holds, and whether it looks at the world or copies another record. The till lines are every sale as scanned: sales, not demand. Book stock is what the system believes is on hand, and it never looks at the shelf. *The AI uses it to pick the right record, and to know which numbers are copies.*
3. **KPIs.** The agreed way to work out each number. On-shelf availability is the share of shelf checks that found the item on the shelf – never worked out from book stock. *The AI uses the agreed formula instead of making one up.*
4. **Rules.** How the work must go, in plain words, each tied to the records it is about. Demand is not sales, and if asked how many shoppers left without an item, say that no record holds them. *The AI follows them – including when to say "no record".*
5. **Worked examples.** Questions with checked answers, such as units sold by item and month. *The AI copies a pattern known to be right.*

There is a sixth form, meant for machines rather than for an AI that answers questions: the same things and rules in a standard format a computer can check, such as OWL and SHACL. In the technical detour of this season, checks like these found ten problems in June's records. They are for testing the data. As you will see, they did much less when pasted in front of an AI.

## Did it change what the AI said?

I asked four questions about 5 kg atta in June 2026. Two have answers in the records: 567 bags were sold, and staff found the atta on the shelf in six of eight checks – 75%. Two have no answer anywhere: how many shoppers walked away without a bag, and the lost sales. The honest reply to those two is "no record holds this".

Without the knowledge layer, NVIDIA's model gave real numbers from the wrong records: 2 shoppers, which were really the two empty-shelf checks, and 3 lost bags, which were really bags thrown away as damaged. With it, the same model said no record holds them, and gave what the records do show – the two empty checks and the 567 bags sold.

A smaller model moved to the right records once it had the knowledge layer – the 567 bags came from the till – but it still answered what no record can answer. Asked how many shoppers walked away, it gave the 567 bags sold.

Then I gave the same test to Claude's three models. Sonnet said "no record" with or without the knowledge layer. The smallest, Haiku, took a sales forecast and called it 43 to 84 shoppers, even with the knowledge layer in front of it. A forecast is a guess about the future. It is not a count of people. When I gave the same models the notes that Sonnet and Opus had written themselves, Haiku did not make that mistake in any of its four tries. It still gave a rough lost-sales number unless told not to.

## What kind of writing worked?

Plain words, tied to the real records. The rule that changed NVIDIA's answers did not stop at "demand is not sales". It said where sales live – the till lines – and what to do when asked about shoppers who left: say that no record holds them.

I also gave 14 AI models the knowledge as formal OWL and SHACL files instead of plain words. The file written by hand did about as well as files a machine wrote from the tables. A few smaller models went looking for tables named after the file's ideas – a "sale" table, a "demand" table – that do not exist, found nothing, and said "no record" to everything, even to the bags sold. One plain sentence helped more than any of the files: *When no record holds the answer, say "no record" and give no number.*

**A knowledge layer is the business written down for an AI – and plain words, tied to the real records, work best.**

So in summary – a knowledge layer holds the words, the records, the KPIs, the rules and worked examples, and the AI uses each one differently. It changed where the AI looked and what it was willing to say. But the model has to be strong enough to use it. The rule I would give any AI agent – **When no record holds the answer, say so, and if you estimate, say how.**
