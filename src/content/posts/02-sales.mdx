When sales of a product fall 59% in a week, what is the first question you ask? I believe most of us ask what the shoppers did. Very few of us ask whether the shoppers could buy.

## What is it?

Sales is the first number in almost every business review. For one item, it is what the tills scanned this week against last week. For a chain, the same idea becomes [like-for-like sales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Same-store_sales), which compares only the stores open in both periods, usually against the same period a year earlier.

Sales drive the forecast, next week's order, the space an item gets on the shelf and the conversation with the supplier.

## What did it say at Store 014?

Store 014 is a supermarket in Jayanagar, Bengaluru, that exists only in a simulation I built. I followed eleven everyday items through June 2026, hour by hour, so I know what shoppers wanted as well as what they bought.

It is Monday 15 June, and the weekly trading review opens on 5 kg bags of whole wheat atta. Sales are down 59%, from 217 bags to 88. The 5 kg pack's share of atta sales has dropped from 40% to 25%, so the obvious reading is that shoppers are moving to the 1 kg pack. The forecast eases a little, and the review moves on.

Now let us look at the shoppers. The number of bags they came for fell 34% that week. Part of that fall is normal, because households stock up in the first week of the month. The rest of the drop in sales was the shelf, which stood empty from Wednesday afternoon to Saturday morning.

**Sales fell 59%. The bags shoppers came for fell 34%.**

## What could it not see?

A till records a sale, not a shopper. A shopper who finds an empty shelf leaves no row anywhere, so for those days sales stopped measuring demand and started measuring the shelf.

The pack mix misleads the same way. In the hours the 5 kg bag was on the shelf, its share hardly moved. It fell because there was no 5 kg to buy, and some shoppers took another pack instead. An AI assistant given only the sales and stock tables can easily reach the same conclusion as the review, because nothing in those tables says the shelf was empty.

This is not a quirk of my simulation. As per [a worldwide study of grocery out-of-stocks](https://www.fmi.org/newsroom/news-archive/view/2002/10/14/new-industry-study-explores-root-causes-of-global-out-of-stock-dilemma), the average out-of-stock rate is 8.3%, and 31% of shoppers who face one go to another store. [Economists who tracked availability in 54 vending machines](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14315) found that demand estimates change significantly once stock-outs are taken into account.

## How do you catch it?

- Before comparing two weeks, look for long runs with no sales while the system shows stock. For an item that sells every hour, a silent afternoon is a question.
- Compare the weeks again without those hours, or compare estimates of demand instead of sales.
- Read the pack mix only for the hours when every pack was on the shelf.

So in summary, before you explain a drop in sales by what shoppers did, check whether they could buy. The rule I would give any AI agent that reads sales – **Before you compare sales across periods, take out the hours the shelf was empty.**
