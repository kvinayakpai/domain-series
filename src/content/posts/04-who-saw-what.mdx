The three posts in this series followed three failures through one month at Store 014, the fictional supermarket in Jayanagar, Bengaluru, that the series' synthetic world simulates hour by hour. Bags of 5 kg atta sat in the wrong bay while their shelf stood empty for three days ([retail](../retail/)). A pallet line arrived holding 1 kg packs where every record said 5 kg bags ([logistics](../logistics/)). Twenty-four customers paid twice when the tills gave up on UPI payments their banks had already sent ([payments](../payments/)).

Each time, the dashboards were green, the data tests passed, and an AI assistant given the schema said nothing was wrong. Each time, two of the store's own records disagreed within hours, and nothing joined them.

This post puts the three on one map. It asks two questions any data team can answer about its own records: which of them are copies of each other, and which of them look at the world?

## Hours by joining, days by waiting

<TimeToFind />

Five kg atta stopped selling three hours after its shelf emptied on 10 June, with 56 bags on the books; staff found the bags in the wrong bay 66 hours after. On 23 June, the query traced below names the wrong pallet line four and a half hours after the 5 kg shelf emptied; the manager's count found it 66 hours after. Matching each timed-out UPI payment to the provider's next-morning file finds an extra charge a median 14 hours after the debit. Complaints took a median 48, and three customers never complained.

The [opener](../why-domain/) argued that every KPI should carry two numbers, as reported and as observed, and one more: how long the gap takes to find. For June at Store 014 that number is hours if you join the records, and days if you wait.

## Join the records two at a time

Take every pair of June's records that share a key, and count how often they agree.

<JoinTable />

Eight joins agree on every row. The shipping notice matches the pick confirmations on all 70 lines, and the goods receipts match the notice on all 70. The book stock equals yesterday's book, plus receipts and deliveries, minus sales and write-offs, plus counts, on all 297 item-days. All 18,384 UPI payments the tills approved are successes in the provider's report, for the same amount.

They agree because one record was written from the other, or both from the same event. The notice is written from the pick confirmations, the receipt by scanning a pallet label against the notice, and the book stock from the receipts and the sales. A data test on such a join can only pass, and that is why every pipeline in this series was green. Those tests are still worth running, because they catch lost loads and broken keys. They cannot catch a wrong pallet, because the mistake is in both records.

Four joins disagree, and each of June's failures sits on one of them. The book stock disagrees with 21 of 25 stock counts, most by a few units; the count on 26 June found 63 bags of 5 kg atta that were not there. It disagrees with two of 88 shelf audits, and one of them, at 11:00 on 12 June, found the 5 kg shelf empty with 55 bags on the books. One goods receipt in ten did not survive the next count: the 22 June line. And 32 of 48 timeouts disagree with the provider's report: the till said failed, and the customer's bank had sent the money.

On each of those four joins, one side looks at the world: someone counting the shelf and the backroom, someone looking at the shelf, the customer's bank.

## Who saw what

Draw the records as a map: what writes each one, what it looks at in the world, and which KPI reads it.

<RecordMap />

Three things stand out. The KPIs read copies: OTIF reads the goods receipts, on-shelf availability reads the book stock, and payment success reads the till payments. The records that look at the world sit at the bottom of the map, and no headline KPI reads them. And one thing had no record looking at it at all: what was on a pallet. None of June's 30 pallets was weighed or case-scanned, all 70 picks were confirmed by scanning the slot, and all 70 receipts by scanning the label.

The map is domain knowledge, written down. Most of its edges can be drawn from the schema and the joins, and W3C's PROV standard has names for them: a record *was generated by* an activity, and *was derived from* another record. The edges to the world, which record looks at which thing and how much of it, are the part only someone who knows the domain can draw. They are also the part an agent needs most.

## Across the shelf and the truck

A map earns its keep when a question crosses a boundary between departments. At 8 pm on Tuesday 23 June, the store's records show 5 kg atta silent since 4 pm, with 63 bags on the books. Follow the map from there.

<TracePath />

One query, across five records and two buildings, names line 1 of shipment SHP-260622, and the answer key agrees: it held 160 packs of 1 kg. It does so at 20:00 on Tuesday, 38 hours before the warehouse's own corrections pair up and 61 hours before the store's count.

Neither domain's rules get there alone that evening. The retail rules see an empty shelf with stock on the books. The scripted replenishment agent from the retail post, rerun with those rules, asked for a shelf check at 22:00 that Tuesday. At 08:00 on Wednesday staff found the shelf and the backroom empty, the agent corrected the book, and the store reordered that night: 22 bags lost instead of 72. It never learned why. Its check counted the 5 kg bay; the 160 extra packs of 1 kg, the warehouse's slots and a delivery the scorecard still counts as in full were outside its rules. The logistics rules pair the warehouse's corrections, and the second one comes on Thursday.

## What the ledger showed

<LedgerView />

Add the ledger to the records that agree with each other. The bags customers wanted and could not buy appear in no record at all. The four corrections from the wrong pallet net to ₹80. The double charges arrived as UPI credits like any other, and the ones nobody claimed sat in suspense.

## Give the agent the map

Agents act on records, so an agent's mistakes are its records' mistakes, made at the speed of software. In this series the same scripted assistant answered seven questions in each domain, first given the schema and then given a domain layer: definitions, rules and views built from the records. The same replenishment agent ran forty Junes with two readings of the KPIs.

<AgentsAcross />

Outside this synthetic world the evidence points the same way. The insurance benchmark that dbt Labs reran in 2026, quoted in the [opener](../why-domain/), was built in 2023 by Juan Sequeda and colleagues at data.world to test this question. GPT-4 answered 16% of its questions correctly from the SQL tables, and 54% when the same data was given to it as a knowledge graph of the business. In 2024, checking each generated query against the business's ontology raised accuracy to 72%, with another 8% answered "I don't know".

A domain layer for an agent is this map, as data:

- every record, the event that writes it, and what it looks at in the world;
- every join, and how often it agrees;
- every KPI, the record it reads, and the observation that can contradict it;
- the rules for reading a silence or a timeout as a question.

Store 014's map is [free to download as JSON](../data/india-grocery/store-014-map.json), next to the records it describes, and the [KPI page](../kpis/) gives each of the series' eighteen KPIs its blind spot and the rule an agent needs before acting on it.

## For data and AI leaders: five questions to ask your team

1. For each KPI an agent acts on, which record does it read, and is that record a copy?
2. Which of our joins have never disagreed, and do we know why?
3. What in our business does no record look at?
4. When two departments' records disagree, who joins them, and how soon?
5. Can our agents read the map: what each record sees, and what can contradict it?
