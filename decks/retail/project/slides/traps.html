Why the data could not say so

Sales are censored demand

A till records a sale, not a shopper. When the shelf is empty, sales measure the shelf, and every model trained on them inherits the gap: forecasts, elasticities, mix shares.

Book stock is a belief

It is computed: the last count plus receipts, minus sales and write-offs. It agreed with every transaction and was wrong about the shelf for three days. No data test looks at the shelf.

The holdout is censored too

Scored on recorded sales, the forecast looks 13% too high. Scored on the shoppers who came, it is 8% too low. The better a model fits the holdout, the worse it gets.

Conlon and Mortimer (2013) on demand estimation when availability changes; DeHoratius and Raman (2008) on inventory record inaccuracy.

Three traps sit under this week, and anyone who has built on transaction data has stepped in at least one. Sales are censored demand: a till records a sale, not a shopper, so when the shelf is empty, sales measure the shelf, and every model trained on sales inherits the gap. Book stock is a belief, not an observation: it is computed from the last count, receipts, sales and write-offs, which is why every reconciliation test passes; it agreed with every transaction and was wrong about the shelf for three days. And the evaluation data is censored too: scored against recorded sales the store’s forecast looks 13% too high, scored against the shoppers who came for the item it is 8% too low. The holdout set has the same hole as the training set.
